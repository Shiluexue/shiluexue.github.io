I design search experiences that understand what people mean — not just what they type.

Search is where I've built my deepest expertise. To me, search is not simply about matching a query with relevant results. It needs to understand the real need behind what a traveler says, organize relevant supply across different travel businesses, surface connections they may not have considered, and help them move toward a better decision.

Sometimes that means getting users to exactly what they asked for as quickly as possible. Sometimes it means connecting their search with relevant hotels, attractions, destinations, products, or content across the travel journey. And when users don't yet know exactly what they want, search can also spark inspiration, help them narrow down possibilities, and gradually turn vague interest into a clearer travel plan.

More recently, I've been shaping how AI becomes part of this search experience — moving beyond simply generating answers toward understanding tasks, supporting decisions, and connecting inspiration with actionable travel supply.

07 — BEYOND THE RÉSUMÉ

Beyond the Résumé

A few questions I get asked often — with answers I actually mean.

How did you get into design?

I started in engineering.

Engineering taught me to understand how technology works; design taught me to ask why it should exist for people in the first place.

That combination still shapes how I work today — especially in AI, where I often sit between what technology can do and what people actually need.

What role should AI play in a product?

AI that stays invisible.

The best AI in search doesn't announce itself. It works behind the scenes — understanding context, uncovering unspoken needs, and helping people decide with less effort.

How do you use AI as a designer?

To give my team expert-level critique on demand.

Most AI critique tools stop at the surface — spacing, contrast, copy. What UX designers actually need is harder: Is this solving the right problem? What creative alternatives haven't we explored? How does each option shift the experience for different user segments? What trade-offs are we quietly accepting?

I turned my instinct for direction, creative alternatives, and cross-segment trade-offs into an AI critique skill. Designers get sharper questions, wider options, and critique that pushes their thinking past the first solution — not just polishes it.

What kind of designer are you becoming?

One who makes other people better at solving the problem, too.

I still love getting deep into interaction details, but increasingly, I'm interested in building the frameworks, standards, and shared understanding that help a team make better decisions without depending on one designer.

For me, growing as a designer means moving from solving individual problems well to making good solutions more repeatable, scalable, and easier for others to build on.
